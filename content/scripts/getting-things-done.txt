Right now, somewhere in the back of your mind, there's probably a nagging list: a bill to pay, an email to answer, a gift to buy, a conversation you've been putting off. David Allen calls these open loops, and his classic book Getting Things Done argues that they quietly drain our focus and create stress. His solution is a complete system for capturing, organising and acting on everything competing for your attention. In this deep dive, we'll walk through the five steps of GTD, explore why they work, and see how you can start using them today.

The core insight behind the method is that your mind is for having ideas, not holding them. When you try to remember everything you need to do, your brain keeps reminding you at random, often at unhelpful moments, like remembering you need batteries while you're in the shower. Allen's goal is what he calls a mind like water: calm, responsive, and ready to engage with whatever comes up. To get there, you need to move every commitment out of your head and into a trusted external system.

The first step is capture. Collect everything that has your attention into a small number of inboxes. That might be a notebook, a notes app, a physical tray on your desk, and your email inbox. Allen recommends an initial mind sweep, writing down every task, idea, worry and project, big or small. People often find this list surprisingly long, and surprisingly relieving.

The second step is clarify. Process each item one by one, asking a key question: is this actionable? If not, it's either trash, reference material to file, or something for a someday-maybe list. If it is actionable, decide the very next physical action. Not plan party, but text Sam to ask for available dates. Here comes one of GTD's most popular rules: if an action takes less than two minutes, do it now. Otherwise, delegate it or defer it to your lists. And if the outcome requires more than one action, it's a project, and it goes on your project list.

The third step is organise. Allen suggests a set of lists: projects, next actions, waiting for, and someday-maybe, plus a calendar reserved strictly for things that must happen on a specific day or time. Next actions are often grouped by context, such as at computer, on phone, errands or at home. This means that when you're in a particular situation, you can glance at the relevant list and immediately see what you can do. If you're waiting at the dentist with your phone, your calls list is right there.

The fourth step is reflect, centred on the weekly review. Once a week, you get clear by emptying your inboxes, get current by updating your lists and checking your calendar, and get creative by considering new projects and ideas. Many people say the weekly review is the heart of GTD. Without it, the system goes stale, trust erodes, and your brain starts holding onto things again.

The fifth step is engage: actually doing the work. Allen suggests choosing what to do based on context, time available, energy, and priority. If you have ten minutes and low energy before a meeting, pick a quick, simple task from your list instead of starting your hardest project.

Beyond the daily mechanics, GTD encourages thinking at different altitudes, from immediate actions to projects, areas of responsibility, goals, vision and purpose. This helps ensure that your lists reflect what genuinely matters, not just what's urgent.

So how do you get started? This week, do a full mind sweep and get everything onto paper. Then process each item with the question, what's the next action? Set up your basic lists in whatever tool you'll actually use; a notebook works just fine. Apply the two-minute rule ruthlessly. And put a recurring weekly review in your calendar, perhaps Friday afternoon, treating it as a meeting with yourself.

To recap: open loops cost attention and create stress. GTD's five steps, capture, clarify, organise, reflect and engage, give you a trusted system so your mind can let go. Defining concrete next actions turns vague worries into doable steps. And the weekly review keeps the whole system alive. The result isn't just getting more done. It's feeling in control, and having the clear, calm mind to focus on what's in front of you.
